\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter evaluates the classification pipeline of \Cref{ch:classification} under Leave-One-Run-Out cross-validation (LORO-CV, \Cref{sec:eval_protocol}) on four configurations: the five-class campus development dataset (Dataset~A, \Cref{sec:initial_dev_dataset}), its three-class restriction on the same multi-speed windows, the three-class low-constant-speed campus subset (Dataset~B), and the primary three-class farm dataset (\Cref{sec:farm_dataset}). Results are reported as macro-averaged $F_1$ with its $95\,\%$ confidence interval and worst-fold value across LORO folds; the robustness score $r = \overline{F_1^{\mathrm{macro}}} - 0.5\,\sigma_{F_1^{\mathrm{macro}}}$ (\Cref{eq:robustness}) is used only as a tie-breaker in the hyperparameter sweeps.

\Cref{sec:res_campus,sec:res_farm} present the per-dataset headline findings; the velocity--vibration confound of \Cref{sec:bg-vel} is treated within \Cref{sec:res_campus} (the multi- vs.\ low-speed campus subsets) and \Cref{sec:res_models} (a speed-only feature ablation across all three datasets). \Cref{sec:res_models} also consolidates the cross-dataset robustness diagnostics (sensor dropout and noise injection), paired-Wilcoxon significance testing, the tuning and top-MI vs.\ all-features comparisons, and the campus-vs-farm shared-class gap. \Cref{sec:res_transition} finally evaluates the sliding-window classifier as a transition detector, with both the implicit $n_{\text{stable}}$ rule and the CUSUM detector of \Cref{sec:bg-transition}.

\section{Test Dataset: Campus Development Route}
\label{sec:res_campus}

The campus dataset was recorded to develop the pipeline against varied outdoor surfaces before the farm campaign (\Cref{sec:initial_dev_dataset}). It is evaluated here in three nested configurations, each isolating a single source of difficulty:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Dataset~A (5-class)} --- all windows, all five labels (\texttt{grass}, \texttt{smooth\_terrain}, \texttt{dry\_dirt\allowbreak \_track}, \texttt{muddy\_dirt\_track}, \texttt{cobblestone}). The full multi-speed problem; all six classifier families (LR, RF, XGBoost, SVM, MLP, CNN) are evaluated.
    \item \emph{Dataset~A (3-class)} --- the same multi-speed windows restricted to the three classes well populated across every session (\texttt{grass}, \texttt{smooth\_terrain}, \texttt{dry\_\allowbreak dirt\_track}). Only the label set differs from the 5-class problem; the speed range and windowing are identical. Only the four classical baselines (LR, RF, XGBoost, SVM) are evaluated, since this configuration serves purely as a controlled comparison point for isolating the class-imbalance effect from the speed-regime effect.
    \item \emph{Dataset~B (3-class)} --- the same three classes further restricted to the low-constant-speed regime of \Cref{eq:dataset_B_mask}. Because the campus robot was already operated at moderate speed most of the time, roughly $70\,\%$ of the 3-class Dataset~A windows already satisfy the mask, so Dataset~B sits strictly inside Dataset~A. It also provides a parallel comparison point with the farm dataset (\Cref{sec:res_farm}), since the class count and speed regime match. All six classifier families are evaluated.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Dataset~A, 5 classes --- the full problem.} \Cref{tab:campus_master} lists the best configuration of each classifier family on Dataset~A. The six families fall within a narrow $F_1^{\mathrm{macro}}$ band of $0.728$ (SVM) to $0.766$ (MLP), a spread well below the within-family cross-fold standard deviation of $0.12$--$0.15$; the paired Wilcoxon test (\Cref{fig:significance_campus}) returns no pair with $p < 0.05$. The stratified dummy scores $0.20$ macro-$F_1$ against the $0.20$ chance level for five classes, confirming that the LORO splits do not themselves leak class structure. The cross-fold variance is driven mostly by the two rare classes discussed next.

\paragraph{Per-class failures driven by class imbalance and route effects.} The $F_1 \approx 0.77$ headline is heavily diluted by two minority classes (as seen in \Cref{tab:window_counts}) whose per-class scores collapse on the worst folds. \Cref{tab:campus_perclass} reports per-class MLP numbers, representative of the other classical models: \texttt{cobblestone} reaches only $F_1 = 0.31$ (folds at $0.08$ and $0.55$), and \texttt{muddy\_dirt\_track} reaches $F_1 = 0.43$ with a high recall variance and worst-fold value of $0.17$. The fold-quality diagnostic (\Cref{tab:fold_quality_campus} ) explains the collapse: both classes are recorded almost exclusively on a single session each (cobblestone on Run~B of the 9~March log and on the 26~March log; muddy dirt track on the two snowy February sessions), so when the dominant run becomes the test fold the model has essentially no training data for that class. Speed conditioning compounds the problem: each minority class shows a train-test speed total-variation distance of $0.56$--$0.86$, visible in the per-class speed-bin distributions of \Cref{fig:speed_bins}: \texttt{muddy\_dirt\_track} and \texttt{smooth\_terrain} are recorded almost exclusively at high speed, while the other cluster at the operating speed. The behaviour is a dataset-collection limitation, not a model failure, and motivates dropping the two classes in the next configuration.

\paragraph{Dataset~B, 3 classes --- isolating the speed-regime effect.} Applying the low-constant-speed mask of \Cref{eq:dataset_B_mask} on top of the 3-class label set produces Dataset~B. Because roughly $70\,\%$ of the 3-class Dataset~A windows already satisfy the mask, any headline difference reflects the residual effect of removing the high-speed and accelerating windows. \Cref{tab:campus_A3_vs_B3} reports the paired A~$\to$~B comparison: every classical model gains $0.003$--$0.026$ macro-$F_1$ consistently in direction, with the worst-fold $F_1$ also improving. The gain is an order of magnitude smaller than the $\sim 0.20$ class-imbalance step, so class imbalance dominates the headline gap. Two caveats apply: the paired comparison only measures the marginal contribution of the $\sim 30\,\%$ of windows the mask removes, and the speed range on the campus logs is itself bounded by the session driving profiles. A stronger statement about the velocity--vibration confound (\Cref{sec:bg-vel}) would require a dedicated wide-speed-range experiment; from the present data the speed effect is minimal but present and consistent in direction at the sampled operating point.

\paragraph{Reading the three configurations together.}
The three-configuration sweep separates the two sources of headline improvement entangled in the 5-class~$\to$~B comparison. Dropping the minority classes is worth $\sim 0.20$ macro-$F_1$ on the same multi-speed windows; restricting to the low-constant-speed regime on top of that buys a further $0.003$--$0.026$, an order of magnitude smaller. \Cref{fig:campus_decomposition} visualises the decomposition with Random Forest \texttt{all, unweighted} held constant across the three datasets ($+0.20$ and $+0.019$). The farm dataset analysed next is the more realistic operating point; the $\sim 0.12$ gap between the Dataset~B and farm results is attributable to the harder class structure of the farm (\texttt{grass}--\texttt{soil} confusion) rather than to a residual speed effect.

\section{Farm Dataset: Primary Classification Result}
\label{sec:res_farm}

The farm dataset is the primary target of this thesis: three terrain classes (\texttt{dirt\_track}, \texttt{grass}, \texttt{soil}) recorded across five runs on two field days at the low near-constant speed used in production (\Cref{sec:farm_dataset}).

\paragraph{Headline performance.}
\Cref{tab:farm_master} lists the best configuration of each classifier family. The three top classical baselines (SVM, LR, XGBoost) all reach $F_1 \approx 0.86$ within overlapping $95\,\%$ CIs; the CNN comes within $\sim 0.02$ on the raw five-channel signal but with a larger spread, and the MLP and random forest sit slightly behind. The paired Wilcoxon test (\Cref{fig:significance_farm}) returns no pair with $p < 0.05$, so \Cref{tab:farm_master} should be read as a single $0.84$--$0.87$ cluster. The stratified dummy at $0.32$ macro-$F_1$ matches the $0.33$ chance level for three classes, confirming the LORO splits do not leak class structure.

\section{Model Comparison}
\label{sec:res_models}

This section cross-cuts the per-dataset results and consolidates the cross-dataset diagnostics. It addresses three questions: how the classifier families compare under a controlled head-to-head, how much of the campus-vs-farm gap is attributable to model choice versus dataset structure, and how gracefully the pipeline degrades under sensor failure, additive feature noise, and removal of the explicit speed columns.

\paragraph{All families are within one cross-fold standard deviation of each other, but the ranking is structured.}
On the farm the SVM and LR are nominally best; on the campus 5-class problem, the MLP edges ahead, and the SVM drops to the bottom of the cluster; on both 3-class campus configurations (Datasets~A and~B), the SVM and LR top the list (\Cref{tab:campus_master,tab:campus_A_3class,tab:campusB_master,tab:farm_master}). The SVM/LR are consistently high across every 3-class problem regardless of speed regime, while the 5-class problem with its two rare classes is the only configuration in which max-margin methods lose ground, a pattern more consistent with class structure than with model capacity. In every case, however, the within-dataset top-to-bottom spread is $\sim 0.05$ macro-$F_1$, smaller than the cross-fold standard deviation of any single family, and the paired-Wilcoxon test returns no model pair with $p < 0.05$ on any of the three datasets (\Cref{fig:significance_campus,fig:significance_farm}). At this dataset size ($\sim 10^4$ windows per dataset) and at the LORO operating point, the choice of model family is a second-order effect compared to the choice of dataset, feature set, and (on the campus) speed regime, consistent with the general finding in the IMU classification literature that classical features and classical baselines remain competitive at moderate dataset sizes (\Cref{sec:bg-features-ml}).

\vspace{-8mm}
\paragraph{Deep models match the engineered-feature baselines within cross-fold noise.}
The 1D-CNN consumes only the raw $5 \times 200$ window and reaches $F_1 = 0.849 / 0.757 / 0.952$ across the farm, campus 5-class, and Dataset~B problems, within one standard deviation of the top classical baseline on every dataset. Its worst-fold $F_1$ is consistently lower than that of the classical baselines ($0.72 / 0.57 / 0.90$), suggesting that the CNN is more sensitive to the cross-fold distribution shift than the engineered-feature pipeline; the engineered features act as a soft regularisation against the kind of shifts LORO exposes. The MLP, which consumes the same engineered features as the classical baselines, sits at $F_1 \approx 0.84 / 0.77 / 0.97$: it narrowly edges out the classical leaders on the harder 5-class campus problem, where its higher capacity (and explicit class-weighted loss) appears to benefit the rare classes, but trails them by $\sim 0.02$ on the two cleaner 3-class problems. At this dataset size the deep models neither help nor hurt the headline number but do increase the fold-to-fold variance.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter tuning and feature-set choice are marginal.}
Tuned baselines (\texttt{08\_b\allowbreak ase\_tuning}) recover essentially the same macro-$F_1$ as the untuned baselines on all three datasets, with the randomised search settling on near-identical hyperparameters on the farm and campus 3-class problems and only diverging on the harder 5-class campus problem (\Cref{tab:tuned_hparams}). The tuned-vs-untuned $\Delta F_1$ is within $\pm 0.01$ in every case: below the cross-fold standard deviation. The top-MI vs.\ all-features choice is dataset-dependent: on the campus the top-MI shortlist edges ahead (LR, SVM, XGBoost all reach their best $F_1$ in the top-MI configuration on both A and B), while on the farm the full feature table wins by $\sim 0.03$. The farm's winning configuration includes the cross-sensor correlation features that distinguish \texttt{grass} from \texttt{soil}; on the more heterogeneous campus surface mix, a small handful of the most discriminative features suffices.

\paragraph{The campus-vs-farm gap is dominated by dataset, not by model.}
\Cref{fig:per_class_farm_vs_campus} aligns the two shared class pairs across the farm and campus three-class problems for all four classical baselines. The \texttt{dirt\_track} pair is essentially solved on both sites; the farm \texttt{grass} class, by contrast, hovers at $F_1 = 0.71$--$0.76$ across every model family, while the campus \texttt{grass} reaches $0.94$--$0.98$. The $\sim 0.20$ farm--campus gap on \texttt{grass} exceeds any model-family difference on either dataset, and the explanation lies in the local class structure rather than the choice of classifier.

The campus three-class subset (\texttt{smooth\_terrain}, \texttt{grass}, \texttt{dry\_dirt\_track}; \Cref{sec:terrain_classes_and_labels}) has no analogue of the farm's \texttt{soil} class, so its three classes span the full roughness spectrum: smooth asphalt, vegetated lawn, compacted dirt, and every pairwise contrast in it is large. The farm triple, by contrast, contains the much harder \texttt{grass}--\texttt{soil} pair: short grass on firm soil and bare loose soil sit close together on the same roughness spectrum and produce overlapping low-amplitude, low-frequency chassis motion. The campus and farm \texttt{grass} are also physically different surfaces (urban park lawn vs.\ farm grass at the field edge). Still, the dominant factor is the class-triple geometry: the campus problem is intrinsically easier because its three classes are further apart in vibration signature, not because the campus classifier is better.

\paragraph{Sensor-dropout robustness.}
A central question for an intrinsic-only pipeline is which sensor channel group carries the discriminative information and how gracefully the pipeline degrades if one fails. Each group was disabled in two modes: \emph{test-time zero}, simulating a transient sensor failure by zero-ing the corresponding columns on the held-out fold only, and \emph{full ablation}, simulating permanent removal by retraining without those columns. \Cref{fig:dropout_compare} reports the test-time-zero outcomes across the three datasets.

Two findings emerge. First, the contrast between the two modes is most informative on the farm: a transient zero of either inertial channel group at test time is catastrophic (\Cref{fig:dropout_compare} Farm), but the same channel removed permanently with retraining costs only $\Delta F_1 = -0.02$ for the accelerometer and $-0.01$ for the gyroscope. The model can express the terrain signature in either inertial channel group alone: pitch/roll rates and the vertical accelerometer share the same physical excitation, but if it has been trained on both and then loses one without warning, the standardised zero values fall far outside the in-distribution feature space and the decision boundary breaks. Odometry is essentially free in both modes, confirming that the speed-normalised vibration features already absorb its discriminative information.

Second, the campus-vs-farm balance differs. On the farm, the accelerometer and gyroscope are roughly co-equal; on the campus, the gyroscope is dominant in both datasets, and the asymmetry sharpens further on the 3-class Dataset~B, where the accelerometer is essentially redundant. The plausible reading lies in the surface mix: the campus includes extremes of stiffness (smooth asphalt vs.\ cobblestone) whose discriminative signature is dominated by the pitch and roll modes that the gyroscope captures, whereas the farm classes differ as much in vertical-acceleration amplitude as in chassis-rocking, so the discriminative information is split more evenly across the two inertial groups.

\paragraph{Noise sensitivity.}
\Cref{fig:noise_sensitivity} shows the macro-$F_1$ degradation when zero-mean Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ (in units of the standardised feature) is added to the test fold only. The farm classifier degrades most gracefully ($0.84 \to 0.59$ as $\sigma$ goes $0 \to 2$); the five-class campus drops more steeply ($0.75 \to 0.39$); Dataset~B starts the highest ($0.86$) but degrades the fastest ($\to 0.47$). A plausible reading is that Dataset~B's near-saturated clean baseline rests on a few sharp feature contrasts between physically distinct surfaces (smooth asphalt vs.\ vegetated lawn vs.\ compacted dirt), which noise erodes quickly; the farm classifier instead distributes its discriminative weight across many cross-sensor correlations between physically similar classes, which together survive noise better.

\paragraph{Speed-only feature ablation.}
\Cref{sec:res_campus} already showed that restricting the campus dataset to the low-constant-speed regime buys only $0.003$--$0.026$ macro-$F_1$; this ablation probes the velocity--vibration confound from the opposite direction. \Cref{tab:speed_only} compares the best classical baseline on the full pruned feature table against the same baseline trained on the four explicit odometry/speed columns only. \section{Change-Detection-Based Transition Analysis}
\label{sec:res_transition}

\Cref{sec:bg-transition} introduced two ways of declaring a terrain transition from a sliding-window classifier: the implicit $n_{\text{stable}}$ rule, which takes the hard argmax of the per-window posterior and waits for $n_{\text{stable}}$ consecutive consistent windows, and the explicit CUSUM detector, which accumulates the log-likelihood ratio on the soft posterior stream and raises an alarm when the running statistic crosses a threshold $h$ (\Cref{eq:cusum-llr,eq:cusum-recursion,eq:cusum-alarm}). CUSUM decides by itself \emph{whether} a change has occurred and \emph{to which class}, and its threshold $h$ can be calibrated to a target false-alarm rate independently of the underlying classifier, a diagnostic the implicit rule cannot offer.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.} The detector emits $\bigl\{\bigl(k_a^{(j)},\,\hat{c}^{(j)}\bigr)\bigr\}$ from the per-window posterior stream of the best LORO-fold model. Each ground-truth transition $(t_k,\ell_k)$ is paired with the first alarm satisfying $t_k - \delta \le t_{k_a} < t_{k+1}$, with a grace $\delta = 1\,\text{s}$ to allow detections triggered on the last window before the change. The pairing yields one of four outcomes per transition: \textbf{detected} (an alarm exists in the window and $\hat{c} = \ell_k$), \textbf{wrong-class} (an alarm exists but $\hat{c}\neq\ell_k$), \textbf{missed} (no alarm exists in the window), or \textbf{boundary} (insufficient windows remain after $t_k$ to observe a stable detection). Detection delay is $t_{k_a} - t_k$. Alarms outside any expected window are accumulated into a \emph{false-alarm rate} (alarms per minute of stationary signal), which the $n_{\text{stable}}$ evaluation cannot measure intrinsically.

\paragraph{$n_{\text{stable}}$ baseline.} \Cref{tab:transition_nstable} summarises the implicit detector at $n_{\text{stable}} = 5$. On the farm, the CNN and MLP detect every transition ($16/16$) at mean delays of $1.85\,\text{s}$ and $3.58\,\text{s}$; XGBoost and LR detect $94\,\%$ at $4$--$5\,\text{s}$; SVM is the slowest at $13.2\,\text{s}$. On the campus dataset A 5 classes, MLP and RF detect the fastest ($\sim 1\,\text{s}$ at $83\,\%$ detection), but XGBoost, LR, SVM and CNN reach $100\,\%$ detection only after a $\sim 50\,\text{s}$ mean delay, driven by a single uncertain transition on the snowy 23~Feb log where the stable-run rule waits indefinitely for five consistent windows. \Cref{fig:transition_nstable} sweeps $n_{\text{stable}}$ on the farm: mean delay grows linearly while the spurious-flip rate decays inversely. The implicit rule therefore trades delay against false alarms but cannot calibrate to a fixed budget, exactly the limitation the explicit CUSUM detector is intended to address.

\paragraph{Summary.}
On the farm, the CUSUM detector splits the classifier families into two groups. For the slow $n_{\text{stable}}$ detectors (SVM, LR, RF), CUSUM cuts mean detection delay by $\sim 2$--$9\,\text{s}$ at the cost of a $\sim 10$ percentage-point drop in detection rate and the introduction of a small wrong-class rate. For the already-fast MLP and CNN, $n_{\text{stable}}$ remains competitive: their soft posteriors transition cleanly enough that the implicit rule is essentially Bayesian-optimal at the short delay it achieves. Across all models, CUSUM exposes an analytically calibratable threshold and a measurable false-alarm rate, neither of which the implicit rule offers. CUSUM was evaluated only on the farm; the campus $n_{\text{stable}}$ baseline's heavy-tailed delay distribution, where a single uncertain transition drives the mean delay to $\sim 50\,\text{s}$, is exactly the failure mode that an explicit threshold is designed to control, but a quantitative campus comparison is left to future work.
