\section{Motivation}

Robotic intercropping is an emerging agricultural strategy that uses autonomous robots to cultivate multiple crop species in the same field. Field conditions constantly change and a robot that treats the environment as constant can lead to poor navigation, unnecessary stops, or damage to plants. \cite{RoboticIntercroppingProject2026} Therefore, this project aims to equip the robot with the ability to recognize the type of terrain that it experiences while driving, using only the available intrinsic motion sensors. Specifically, accelerometer and gyroscope measurements, together with wheel odometry, are used to capture vibration patterns, impacts, and motion signatures arising from robot–terrain interaction, including transitions between surface types.

The study considers two complementary terrain scenarios. Before the main data collection, a campus route covering smooth pavement, grass, cobblestone, and a dirt track that was recorded under both dry and wet/muddy conditions served as the development environment to build and test the full processing pipeline. The campus route was chosen to mimic different terrains found on the farm, while also including classes that provide an interesting contrast from the others — such as smooth asphalt roads and cobblestones. The clear distinction between asphalt and the softer surfaces also provides a useful reference point for comparison with the primary scenario: an actual farm, where grass, soil, and rough dirt tracks present a harder classification problem due to their greater similarity. Data were collected across multiple repeated runs on each route — four campus runs on separate days and five farm runs across two days — to assess cross-run generalisation. The effect of driving speed was also explicitly investigated by evaluating models on both the full recorded speed range and a lower, more consistent speed regime, with the latter strategy adopted during the main farm data collection. The collected data were labelled by terrain segment, and the project developed and evaluated methods ranging from classical machine learning baselines to a convolutional neural network, also examining transition detection as a harder sub-problem.

Overall, the project does not rely on external perception sensors such as cameras or LiDAR, but instead investigates the limits of proprioceptive terrain recognition. The goal is not to replace exteroceptive sensing, but to explore how intrinsic motion signals can complement it, providing a lightweight, always-available channel for terrain awareness that does not depend on lighting conditions, sensor range, or additional hardware.

\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis addresses the following question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can terrain type be reliably classified from accelerometer, gyroscope, and wheel odometry data collected by a wheeled agricultural robot, without any external perception sensor?}
\end{quote}

Answering this question requires addressing several sub-problems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Designing and executing a repeatable data collection protocol that produces a labelled, multi-session IMU and odometry dataset across representative terrain types, recorded on both a controlled campus development route and an operational farm.
    \item Building a reproducible preprocessing and feature engineering pipeline that converts raw sensor logs into model-ready feature vectors.
    \item Training and comparing multiple classifier families, from classical machine learning baselines to a convolutional neural network, under a Leave-One-Run-Out cross-validation scheme that prevents data leakage between robot runs.
    \item Assessing how driving speed affects classification by evaluating each model on both the full recorded speed range and a lower, more consistent speed regime.
    \item Comparing performance between the campus development scenario, where terrain classes are relatively distinct, and the farm scenario, where surfaces (grass, loose tilled soil, compacted dirt tracks) are more similar and therefore harder to separate, to characterise how terrain similarity affects achievable accuracy with the same pipeline.
    \item Examining transition detection, identifying the boundary between two adjacent terrain segments, as a harder sub-problem than steady-state classification.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Project Scope and Delimitations}

The project focuses exclusively on proprioceptive terrain recognition; no camera, LiDAR, or GPS signals are used. Classification is performed offline on pre-recorded logs, and real-time deployment and embedded implementation are left as future work.

Terrain classes are restricted to surfaces accessible within the two data collection campaigns: a campus route (asphalt, grass, cobblestone, dirt tracks) used to develop the pipeline, and a farm route (grass, soil, dirt track) used as the primary target scenario. Rare events such as deep puddles or steep slopes are not covered. The campus and farm datasets are evaluated as independent classification problems with their own label spaces; direct cross-domain transfer (training on one site and testing on the other) is not performed, as the label sets do not fully overlap and harmonising them would require additional data collection beyond the scope of this thesis.

\label{sec:project-plan}

The project was scoped as an 18-week effort (2 February – 8 June 2026), organised into six phases from initial planning through to final submission. The original weekly plan and milestones are summarised below; the resulting Gantt chart is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:gantt}.

\subsection{Planned Schedule}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 0 — Project Management (W1–2):} define research questions, terrain classes, and success criteria; risk assessment and timeline.
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 — Data Foundation (W2–3):} ingestion pipeline with time alignment, automated quality control, and a labelling system.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 — Proxy Data \& Preprocessing (W2–7):} controlled proxy data collection, resampling and sliding-window extraction, and the first time- and frequency-domain feature set; \emph{Proxy Dataset Freeze}.

          collection and a proxy-to-field domain transfer evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Phase 4 — Validation \& Documentation (W12–15):} robustness
          tests (noise injection, sensor dropout) and the methodology and results
          writing sprint.
    \item \textbf{Phase 5 — Finalisation (W16–18):} introduction, literature
          review, revisions, and submission on 10 June 2026.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deviations from the Plan}

The overall phase structure held, but several elements shifted in scope or
timing as the work progressed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data collection ran longer than planned.} Campus data
          collection was originally scheduled for 15 February – 7 March 2026
          but actually spanned 23 February – 26 March, and the farm collection
          planned for the week of 5–11 April was carried out on 21-28 April.
          In both cases the delays were driven by robot availability and
          maintenance issues, and by weather conditions unsuitable for outdoor
          recording.
    \item \textbf{Proxy data reframed as a campus development route.} The
          original ``proxy'' phase was executed as a four-run campus data
          collection covering asphalt, grass, cobblestone, and dirt-track
          segments, rather than indoor controlled trials. This proved a closer
          analogue to the farm scenario while still offering more separable
          classes for pipeline development. %NEED TO REPHRASE THIS
    \item \textbf{Domain transfer evaluation dropped.} The proxy-to-field
          domain transfer experiment planned for Week 11 was not carried out:
          the campus and farm label spaces only partially overlap (e.g.
          cobblestone and asphalt have no farm counterpart, and the farm
          introduces bare agricultural soil), so a like-for-like train-on-campus /
          test-on-farm evaluation would have required additional aligned data
          collection. The campus and farm datasets are therefore reported as
          two independent classification problems, with comparison drawn at
          the \emph{performance} level rather than via direct transfer.
    \item \textbf{Speed-regime analysis added.} An explicit comparison between
          the full recorded speed range and a lower, more consistent speed
          regime was added as a separate evaluation axis, after early results
          showed that speed variability was a major confound. The low-speed
          strategy was subsequently adopted during the main farm collection.
    \item \textbf{Transition detection added as a sub-problem.} An additional
          analysis of segment-boundary detection was introduced as a harder
          stress test of the pipeline than steady-state classification alone.
    \item \textbf{Robustness work consolidated into the model-comparison
          stage.} The noise-injection and sensor-dropout tests originally
          scoped as a separate Phase 4 activity were folded into the final
          model comparison (notebook \texttt{11\_model\_comparison}), so that
          robustness was reported alongside per-model accuracy rather than
          as a standalone study.
\end{itemize}

These adjustments preserved the project's central question — whether terrain
type can be reliably classified from proprioceptive signals alone — while
reallocating effort from experiments whose preconditions were not met
(label-aligned cross-site transfer) toward analyses that the data actually
supported (speed regimes, transition detection, and side-by-side campus vs.\
farm performance under noise and dropout).
