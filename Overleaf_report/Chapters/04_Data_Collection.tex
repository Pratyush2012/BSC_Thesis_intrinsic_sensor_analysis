\chapter{Data Collection}
\label{ch:collection}

Two outdoor recording campaigns were carried out with Ricbot during this work (\Cref{tab:campaigns}). The earlier \emph{Initial Development} campaign was recorded on and around the DTU Lyngby campus between February and March 2026 and was used to design and validate the processing pipeline itself. The later \emph{Main Agricultural} campaign, recorded at a farm in Taastrup in April 2026, forms the primary dataset on which all classifiers in this thesis are trained, tuned, and cross-validated.

\section{Initial Development Dataset}
\label{sec:initial_dev_dataset}

The Initial Development dataset (stored under \texttt{data/raw/Main} in the project repository) was recorded across four sessions on and around the DTU Lyngby campus between February and March 2026. The intent of this campaign was twofold. First, to gather a first body of labelled IMU and odometry data covering a wide range of outdoor surfaces — asphalt, grass, cobblestone and dirt track — so that the full processing pipeline could be designed and debugged against real, varied data rather than synthetic signals. Second, to use that data to make the practical pipeline-design decisions that the rest of the thesis depends on: the analysis-window length, the minimum amount of useful data required per run for stable Leave-One-Run-Out (LORO) cross-validation, and the approximate target number of runs per terrain class needed before results become reproducible. Because the campus terrain classes (notably asphalt and cobblestone) have no counterpart on the farm, the two campaigns are evaluated as two \emph{independent classification problems}, each with its own label space, rather than via direct train-on-campus / test-on-farm transfer. The campus dataset therefore plays two roles in this thesis: the pipeline-development substrate, and a parallel five-class problem whose results serve as a high-separability reference point against which the harder farm dataset is compared (\Cref{ch:results}).

The four campus sessions are summarised in \Cref{tab:main_sessions}. Each session followed a predefined route covering several terrain types in sequence. The 9 March session was the longest of the four and was driven as two back-to-back routes within one continuous log; in all downstream analysis, it is split at its temporal midpoint and treated as two independent runs: "RunA" and "RunB" from \Cref{ch:classification} onwards. The robot was teleoperated at approximately constant speed within each terrain segment whenever feasible. Weather conditions varied substantially across the campaign, ranging from wet snow to dry pavement, which is one of the reasons this dataset was retained as a stress-test rather than promoted to the primary training set.

\section{Main Agricultural Dataset}
\label{sec:farm_dataset}

The Main Agricultural dataset (stored under \texttt{data/raw/Farm} in the project repository) was recorded on two field days at a working farm during April 2026 and constitutes the primary dataset for this thesis. It was collected after the Initial Development campaign once the preprocessing pipeline, the windowing length, and the LORO cross-validation protocol had been fixed; data was therefore gathered with a specific target number of runs per class in mind, on a single site, under broadly consistent weather conditions, so that the resulting class distributions are balanced enough for stable cross-validated learning.

Five runs were recorded in total, spread across the two field days, following \emph{similar but deliberately non-identical} routes (\Cref{tab:farm_sessions}). Between runs the precise location of the dirt-track, soil and grass segments was varied within the same field, and the order in which the terrain classes were traversed was permuted; this is a deliberate choice that exposes the classifier to a wider range of within-class conditions and prevents it from learning route-specific cues rather than terrain-specific features. Each route was selected so that all three target terrain classes appear multiple times per run with comparable segment durations. All five runs were carried out under dry, sunny conditions with light wind, which keeps environmental confounds approximately constant across runs and means that the remaining inter-run variation is predominantly attributable to the controlled route differences described above and to operator-driven variation in speed, rather than to weather.

For the comparison against the Farm-trained models, the Initial Development data is restricted to a three-class subset containing \texttt{smooth\_terrain}, \texttt{grass}, and \texttt{dry\_dirt\_track}; \texttt{muddy\_dirt\_track} and \texttt{cobblestone} are dropped. This matches the class cardinality of the Main Agricultural dataset ($K = 3$) so that the two classification problems share an identical chance-level baseline and can be compared at the level of macro-F1 without the confound that a five-class problem is intrinsically harder than a three-class one. The two dropped classes are also the weakest candidates for retention: \texttt{cobblestone} has no analogue at the agricultural site, and \texttt{muddy\_dirt\_track} was recorded under transient weather conditions that conflate surface composition with moisture state and yields the smallest sample count after windowing. The retained three labels span the same or a close coarse roughness spectrum (smooth, vegetated, unpaved compacted) that the Farm \texttt{grass}\,/\,\texttt{dirt\_track}\,/\,\texttt{soil} scheme covers, so the two parallel three-class problems are discussed side-by-side in \Cref{ch:results} as comparable-difficulty reference points rather than as a strict train-on-campus / test-on-farm transfer task. All notebooks suffixed with \texttt{\_3class} (\texttt{07a}\,/\,\texttt{07b}\,/\,\texttt{08b}\,/\,\texttt{09b}\,/\,\texttt{10b}) operate exclusively on this reduced subset.

\subsection{Labelling Procedure}

All terrain labels were assigned manually after each session. During every run a continuous video recording of the robot's path was made from a hand-held camera following alongside, so that each terrain transition along the route was captured visually. After the run, the video was reviewed offline and synchronised to the robot's logs by matching a small number of identifiable events (run start, deliberate stops at terrain boundaries) between the video timestamp and the Unix-epoch timestamps in the sensor logs. With that mapping fixed, each visible terrain transition in the video was converted to its corresponding Unix time, and the final $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ ranges for each class were stored in a per-run JSON configuration file (\texttt{labels\_config.json}). \Cref{lst:labels_json} gives a representative example from \texttt{Farm/Run1}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=, caption={Example label configuration (\texttt{labels\_config.json}).}, label={lst:labels_json}]
{
  "grass":      [[1776764662, 1776764730],
                 [1776764798, 1776764844]],
  "dirt_track": [[1776764860, 1776765022]],
  "soil":       [[1776764533, 1776764573],
                 [1776764590, 1776764643]]
}
\end{lstlisting}

Time ranges are absolute Unix epoch seconds matching the timestamp column of the raw log files. Each $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ pair defines a contiguous interval during which the robot was confidently driving on a single terrain type; unlabelled regions (turns, robot stops, ambiguous transitions, or short periods in which the surface could not be identified from the video) are discarded during preprocessing rather than being assigned to a catch-all class.

This labelling scheme is intentionally conservative: it sacrifices a fraction of the recorded data in exchange for cleaner per-window class assignments downstream. Because every window inherits its label from the time range of its mid-point, conservative ranges also prevent transition samples from being silently mixed into a single class --- the dynamics of explicit terrain transitions are studied separately in \Cref{sec:transition_cusum}.
