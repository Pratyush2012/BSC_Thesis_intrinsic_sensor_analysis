\section{Initial Development Dataset}
\label{sec:initial_dev_dataset}

The Initial Development dataset was recorded across four sessions around the DTU Lyngby campus in February–March 2026, with two goals: to gather labelled IMU and odometry data covering a range of outdoor surfaces (asphalt, grass, cobblestone, dry and muddy dirt track) so that the processing pipeline could be designed against real signals; and to fix the pipeline-design decisions that follow: the analysis-window length, the minimum useful run duration for stable Leave-One-Run-Out (LORO) cross-validation, and the target number of runs per terrain class needed before results become reproducible. Because the campus classes (asphalt and cobblestone) have no counterpart on the farm, the two campaigns are evaluated as \emph{independent classification problems} with their own label spaces, rather than via direct train-on-campus / test-on-farm transfer. The campus dataset therefore plays two roles: pipeline-development substrate, and a parallel five-class problem that serves as a high-separability reference for the harder farm dataset (\Cref{ch:results}).

The four sessions are summarised in \Cref{tab:main_sessions}. Each followed a predefined route covering several terrain types in sequence, with the robot operated at approximately constant speed within each terrain segment whenever feasible. The 9 March session was the longest and covered two back-to-back routes within a single log; it is split at its temporal midpoint and treated as two independent runs (\texttt{RunA} and \texttt{RunB}) from \Cref{ch:classification} onwards. Weather varied across the campaign, wet snow on the early February sessions and mostly dry by late March, which is one of the reasons this dataset was retained as a stress-test rather than promoted to the primary training set.

\section{Main Agricultural Dataset}
\label{sec:farm_dataset}

The Main Agricultural dataset was recorded on two field days at a working farm in April 2026 and is the primary dataset for this thesis. It was collected after the Initial Development campaign, once the preprocessing pipeline, windowing length and LORO cross-validation protocol had been fixed; data was therefore gathered with a specific target number of runs per class, on a single site, under broadly consistent weather conditions, so that the resulting class distributions are balanced enough for stable cross-validated learning.

Five runs were recorded in total, spread across the two field days and following \emph{similar but deliberately non-identical} routes (\Cref{tab:farm_sessions,fig:farm_route}). Between runs the precise location of the dirt-track, soil and grass segments was varied within the same field, and the order in which the terrain classes were traversed was permuted; this exposes the classifier to a wider range of within-class conditions and prevents it from learning route-specific cues. Each route was selected so that all three target terrain classes appear multiple times per run with comparable segment durations. All five runs were carried out under dry, sunny conditions with light wind, which keeps environmental confounds approximately constant; the remaining inter-run variation is therefore attributable to the controlled route differences above and to operator-driven variation in speed, not to weather.

For the comparison against the Farm-trained models, the Initial Development data is restricted to a three-class subset containing \texttt{smooth\_terrain}, \texttt{grass} and \texttt{dry\_dirt\_track}; \texttt{muddy\_dirt\_track} and \texttt{cobblestone} are dropped. This matches the class cardinality of the Main Agricultural dataset ($K = 3$) so that the two problems share an identical chance-level baseline and can be compared at the macro-F1 level without the confound that a five-class problem is intrinsically harder than a three-class one. The two dropped classes are also the weakest candidates for retention: \texttt{cobblestone} has no farm analogue, and \texttt{muddy\_dirt\_track} conflates surface composition with transient weather conditions and has the smallest sample count after windowing. The retained three labels span a coarse roughness spectrum (smooth, vegetated, unpaved compacted) that closely matches the Farm \texttt{grass}\,/\,\texttt{dirt\_track}\,/\,\texttt{soil} scheme, so the two parallel three-class problems are discussed side-by-side in \Cref{ch:results} as comparable-difficulty reference points rather than as a strict train-on-campus / test-on-farm transfer task.

\subsection{Labelling Procedure}

All terrain labels were assigned manually after each session. During every run a continuous video recording of the robot's path was made from a hand-held camera following alongside, so that each terrain transition was captured visually. After the run, the video was reviewed offline and synchronised to the robot's logs by matching a small number of identifiable events (run start, deliberate stops at terrain boundaries) between the video and the Unix-epoch timestamps in the sensor logs. With that mapping fixed, each visible terrain transition was converted to its corresponding Unix time, and the final $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ ranges for each class were stored in a per-run JSON configuration file (\texttt{labels\_config.json}). \Cref{lst:labels_json} gives a representative example from \texttt{Farm/Run1}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=, caption={Example label configuration (\texttt{labels\_config.json}).}, label={lst:labels_json}]
{
  "grass":      [[1776764662, 1776764730],
                 [1776764798, 1776764844]],
  "dirt_track": [[1776764860, 1776765022]],
  "soil":       [[1776764533, 1776764573],
                 [1776764590, 1776764643]]
}
\end{lstlisting}

Time ranges are absolute Unix epoch seconds matching the timestamp column of the raw log files. Each $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ pair defines a contiguous interval during which the robot was confidently driving on a single terrain type; unlabelled regions (turns, robot stops, ambiguous transitions, or short periods in which the surface could not be identified from the video) are discarded during preprocessing rather than being assigned to a catch-all class.

This labelling scheme is intentionally conservative: it sacrifices a fraction of the recorded data in exchange for cleaner per-window class assignments downstream. Because every window inherits its label from the time range of its mid-point, conservative ranges also prevent transition samples from being silently mixed into a single class; the dynamics of explicit terrain transitions are studied separately in \Cref{sec:bg-transition}.
