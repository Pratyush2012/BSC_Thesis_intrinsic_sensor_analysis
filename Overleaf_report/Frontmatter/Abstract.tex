\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Autonomous agricultural robots operating in real fields must adapt to the surface they are driving on to navigate safely and avoid damaging crops. This thesis therefore asks whether terrain type can be recognised reliably from a wheeled robot's intrinsic motion sensors alone: accelerometer, gyroscope and wheel-encoder odometry, without any camera, LiDAR or GNSS signal.

A complete proprioceptive terrain-classification pipeline was designed and evaluated end-to-end on \emph{Ricbot}, a small wheeled robot developed at DTU. Data were collected in two outdoor campaigns: a four-run campus campaign (February–March 2026) covering five urban surfaces and used to develop the pipeline, and a primary five-run farm campaign (April 2026) covering the three agriculturally relevant classes \emph{grass}, \emph{tilled soil} and \emph{compacted dirt track}. The pipeline performs automated quality control followed by multi-sensor synchronisation at 100\,Hz, video-aided labelling, removal of low-speed and turning segments, then applies low-pass filtering and windows the data using sliding-window segmentation (2\,s, 50\,\% overlap). We end up extracting 189 time and frequency-domain features per window. Six classifier families, from classical baselines (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost and kernel SVM) to a multilayer perceptron and a 1D convolutional network operating directly on the raw signals, were compared under a Leave-One-Run-Out cross-validation protocol that enforces strict run-disjoint generalisation.

On the primary farm dataset, the best model reached a macro-averaged F\textsubscript{1} of \textbf{0.87}, with all other models within one standard deviation. The narrow spread shows that careful feature engineering keeps classical learners competitive with deep architectures at this data scale. On a matched three-class campus subset the same pipeline reached \textbf{0.98} F\textsubscript{1}, indicating that the residual difficulty on the farm lies in separating physically similar loose-soil surfaces rather than in any limitation of the pipeline itself. A sensor-ablation study identified the \textbf{accelerometer and gyroscope} as the dominant information sources — test-time zeroing cost 0.33 and 0.26 absolute F\textsubscript{1}, respectively — while odometry contributes nothing directly at test time. A transition-detection analysis showed that the neural models flag terrain changes with sub-second median delay and 2 to 13 second mean delay, and a CUSUM detector on the classifier posteriors is added as a principled extension with a tunable false-alarm rate.

Overall, the thesis demonstrates that intrinsic motion signals alone are sufficient to recognise the terrain a small agricultural robot is driving on, that careful feature engineering keeps classical learners competitive with deep architectures at this data scale, and that the accelerometer and gyroscope dominate the information budget. The result supports proprioceptive terrain classification as a lightweight, always-available complement to camera- and LiDAR-based perception in robotic intercropping.
